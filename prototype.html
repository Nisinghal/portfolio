Household Cart for Blinkit

Household Cart is a feature concept for Blinkit. In shared homes, the person who orders ends up coordinating items and chasing money afterwards. I researched how flatmates and partners actually shop together (9 interviews, a 21-response survey), then designed a shared cart where anyone can add, every item shows who added it, nobody is an admin, and the split settles itself. Asking flatmates is optional and never delays the order. Concept project; not affiliated with, or endorsed by, Blinkit.

The problemIn a shared home, one person ends up coordinating the grocery order and chasing everyone for money.
The ideaOne cart the whole household adds to. Whoever orders pays, and the split settles itself.

User flows

One happy flow, then the side flows and edge cases around it. Built means you can try it in the prototype. Open means a decision or a technical check is still pending. Use “Try it” to jump into the matching walkthrough.

Happy flow · a first household order

Discover

An announcement opens when the app starts. A “Shop with flatmates” card stays on Home, and Share offers it too.

Set up

Name the household, add flatmates by phone number, choose whether to split costs.

Add together

Everyone adds from their own phone. Each row says who added it. No approvals.

Order

Whoever is ordering reviews the cart and pays. Checkout itself is unchanged.

Split

A running balance per person, across orders, with the orders behind it.

Settle

If you owe, pay by UPI. If you paid, mark each share as settled.

Side flows

BuiltInvited flatmate
Push on the lock screen → opens straight into the household cart → a dismissible welcome → adds an item (asked once which cart it goes to)

No accept or decline screen. The invite is the cart.

BuiltNudge to add
Push “Priya is ordering” → banner: Add items, or Not tonight → adding stays open until the orderer orders (15 minutes at the very most, never shown) → later adds are saved for the next order, one tap to order them

One nudge only. Skipping is private: the orderer sees when everyone has replied, never who said no.

BuiltOrdering without waiting
Orderer taps “Ask the flat” (her choice, never automatic) → everyone who hasn’t added is asked once → a calm strip says who has replied so far → everyone replied: “You’re good to order” → or she orders any time. If someone is mid-add she gets one prompt: Order now, or Wait a minute

The 15 minutes is a hidden ceiling on asking, never a hold on the orderer. Delivery time starts when she orders.

BuiltMove your cart to the household
Your own cart has items → “Move all to Flat 4B” → they land in the household cart, still tagged as yours → Undo if it was a mistake

One tap, so choosing the wrong cart first costs nothing.

BuiltShort of free delivery
Your own cart is under the minimum → “Move and ask the flat” → your items move and the flat is asked once → their adds can unlock free delivery → order when you’re ready

The delivery fee becomes a reason to ask, not a reason to add things you don’t need. The ask never holds the order.

BuiltSettle up as the payer
Flatmate says they paid → Mark as settled → balance clears. Nudge a flatmate who hasn’t paid, once.

Only the person who paid can mark a share settled.

BuiltChange who’s in the flat
Household setup → remove one person, add another by phone → activity records it

Everyone has the same permissions, including this.

Edge cases · adding

BuiltAdding to the wrong cart

A first-add question, an “Adding to” switch on Home, two stacked cart rows with the active one on top, and counts on the Personal and Household tabs.

BuiltNobody has added anything

“No one’s added anything yet. Add something, or wait — your flatmates can add anytime.”

BuiltIncreasing someone else’s item

The extra unit becomes yours. It shows in your tab, and the All tab shows one row with both initials and their counts. Their own count never changes.

BuiltFiltering by person

Chips show how many each person added. An empty filter says so plainly.

Edge cases · the nudge

BuiltPeeking at the cart

Opening the household checkout sends nothing. The ask only goes out when the orderer taps “Ask the flat”.

BuiltNot tonight

Private: the orderer never sees who skipped, only that everyone has replied. Undo stays until she orders.

BuiltIgnoring it

Nothing happens and no second push is sent. The orderer can still order at any time.

BuiltAdding after the window closes

It closes the moment the orderer orders. The item is saved under “For the next order”, out of today’s total, and the flatmate can order it in one tap.

BuiltSomeone is mid-add when she orders

The orderer sees “Rahul is adding something”. Tapping Place Order asks once: Order now, or Wait a minute (up to 60 seconds). She is never blocked.

BuiltEveryone says “not tonight”

The orderer is told “No one else needs anything” and can pay straight away. No names.

BuiltThe orderer is ready early

Nothing holds Place Order. People who haven’t replied can add until she orders; after that it goes in the next order.

OpenOne-person cart

The rule is no nudge when only one person has added. It’s a rule in the design, not enforced in the prototype.

Edge cases · ordering and money

BuiltOnly personal items in the cart

A normal Blinkit order. Nothing is split.

BuiltFees and the odd rupee

Delivery and handling are split evenly among those who added something. No items means no share. The odd rupee stays with the payer.

BuiltPaid by UPI, not confirmed yet

It reads “Sent via UPI”, with a line saying who will mark it settled.

BuiltSomeone leaves or is removed

Their items leave the cart. Money already owed stays on record.

OpenSplit costs turned off while money is owed

Built as “the balance freezes until everyone is settled.” That is a guess, labelled as one.

OpenPayer-initiated UPI request

Needs a technical check on whether Blinkit can trigger a collect request. Today, the person who owes taps a pre-filled UPI link.

Design decisions

The calls that shaped this concept, each with the reason and what it costs. Open one at a time. Research behind it: 9 interviews and a 21-response survey.

01No admin. Everyone has the same permissions
DecisionAny member can add, remove, rename and manage members. There is no coordinator role.
WhyIn shared homes one person becomes the coordinator by accident, not agreement. An admin would formalise exactly the job people resent. The only things an admin would gate don’t need gating in a home that already chose to share a cart.
Trade-offAnyone can remove anyone. Activity records who did what, so it’s reversible and visible rather than prevented.
02The toggle lives on the Cart screen
DecisionA Personal / Household switch sits at the top of the existing cart. No new tab, no new screen to reach it.
WhyIn Blinkit the cart and checkout are one screen, so the switch sits where the person already decides. It reuses a pattern people already know.
Trade-offTwo modes on one screen risk adding to the wrong cart. Handled by decision 09.
03Checkout stays exactly as it is
DecisionThe payer pays the normal total. The split is a quiet line before paying and a full screen after.
WhyWhoever is ordering shouldn’t face new steps at the moment of paying. The split is a downstream concern, so it lives downstream.
Trade-offThe payer sees only their own share before ordering, not everyone’s. Transparency comes after the order.
04Split by who added what; fees shared equally
DecisionEveryone pays for their own items. Delivery and handling are split evenly among those who added something to that order, so someone with no items pays nothing. The odd rupee stays with the payer.
WhyIt ends the argument about whose milk it was. Shares are calculated, never typed.
Trade-offItems bought for everyone need a rule. Still open.
05A running balance, not a per-order bill
DecisionThe Split screen shows one balance per person across orders, with the orders behind it tucked away.
WhyHouseholds reconcile monthly, the way Splitwise does, not order by order.
Trade-offA single number hides which order caused it. The history is one tap away.
06Only the payer can mark a share as settled
DecisionThe person who owes taps a pre-filled UPI link. The person who paid confirms it later.
WhyBlinkit can’t see a UPI transfer finish, so nobody self-reports. That keeps the balance honest and nobody has to chase.
Trade-offThe payer has one more tap to do. A payer-initiated request is open pending a technical check.
07“Split costs” is a switch, not a household type
DecisionA single on/off switch in setup. There is no Family, Flatmates or Partners choice.
WhyAbout a third of survey respondents don’t split money at all. A switch serves that need without ever asking what kind of household it is, and keeps family households (a different problem) out of v1.
Trade-offTurning it off while money is owed freezes the balance. That’s a guess, not a tested rule.
08The invite opens straight into the cart
DecisionA push notification leads directly to the household cart. There is no accept or decline screen, and a short welcome that can be closed.
WhyThe feature should be invisible until it’s needed. Fewer steps between “Priya added you” and the first item.
Trade-offSomeone can be added without being asked. Leaving is one tap, and money owed stays on record.
09Always show which cart an item is going to
DecisionOne question on the first add, an “Adding to” switch on Home once a household exists, two stacked cart rows (active on top, the other lighter beneath), and counts on the cart tabs.
WhyThe cart and the household cart are different, and the cost of a wrong guess is an item in the wrong place. The cart row never changes meaning silently.
Trade-offHome gets one more row once a household exists. The prototype can switch the row and the question off so they can be tested separately.
StatusNot yet tested with people.
10One ask, no countdown, and the orderer is never held
DecisionWhen the orderer taps “Ask the flat”, people who haven’t added get one push. Nothing is sent just because someone opened the cart. It sets no deadline and creates no pressure: “Priya is putting an order together. Add anything you need, whenever you like.” Her Place Order button is never held. A calm strip tells her who has replied so far, and turns green when everyone has. If someone is actively adding when she taps Place Order, she is asked once: Order now, or Wait a minute. Anything added after she orders is saved for the next order, and ordering it takes one tap.
WhyBlinkit’s promise is speed, and the clock starts when the order is placed. Asking runs alongside checking out, never after it. A visible countdown would be a promise the orderer can break, so flatmates never see one: the system keeps a 15-minute ceiling on the ask, but nobody is told a number. Saying “not tonight” stays private, so nobody is marked as the one who didn’t contribute.
Trade-offIf she orders at once, slow replies miss that order. The one prompt is the only moment she is held, and only when a real person is mid-add. The 15 minutes and the 60-second wait are proposals to test. An alternative worth testing is to ask earlier, when the first person starts the cart.
11Tell people at the start, and keep telling them gently
DecisionAn announcement when the app starts, a dismissible card on Home, an option in Share, and a “NEW” mark on the Household tab.
WhyPeople shopping alone won’t go looking. The feature has to show itself before checkout, while they are still adding things.
Trade-offAn announcement is an interruption. “Not now” is one tap and doesn’t lose the entry points.
12Built in Blinkit’s own system, with its restraint
DecisionReal tokens, components and rhythm. Green only for transactional or active states, blue only for savings, no competing filled buttons, and no blocking modals for cart confirmation.
WhyA feature that looks like a different product feels like an ad. Matching the system is part of making it believable.
Trade-offLess room for expression. Delight is kept to motion, sound and one burst of confetti when a household is created.
13What this concept leaves out on purpose
Out of scopeA default cart or “usual order” that fills itself. Family and joint-family households. Preferences per person.
WhyEach is a different problem. A default cart is about what to order. This concept is about who coordinates and who pays.
Still unansweredWhether a flatmate adds items unprompted (assumed, never observed). Whether the inline toggle beats a separate screen (never tested with people).
14Free delivery is a reason to share, not to pad the cart
DecisionWhen your own cart has items, one tap moves all of it into the household cart. When it’s under the free-delivery minimum, the same card offers “Move and ask the flat”: your items move, and the others are asked once whether they need anything.
WhyA single person’s grocery run often misses the minimum while the flat is short of something anyway. Asking turns the fee into a shared saving and removes the incentive to add things nobody needs. It reuses the ask from decision 10, so it never holds your order.
Trade-offYour items become visible to the flat, tagged as yours. The move can be undone straight away. Whether people are comfortable asking for something this small is untested.
